The Book(s) of Enoch

🕰️ Revised: Nov 2026

By Jake Rogers · Send me feedback

Why a “banned” ancient text keeps echoing into the end times.

Facts first, dots connected, Scripture-centered.

1) Who Enoch was (Bible)

Genesis 5:21–24: Enoch “walked with God; then he was not, because God took him.” He’s an ancestor of Noah and is remembered for not dying in the usual way. Jude 14–15 directly quotes a prophecy attributed to Enoch about the Lord coming with “ten thousands of His holy ones” to judge the wicked.

Checkable: Genesis 5 and Jude are in every Bible; Jude quotes Enoch verbatim.

2) What is “the Book of Enoch” (actually, books)

1 Enoch (Ethiopic): A collection of five parts written and gathered over time, mainly during the Second Temple period. Preserved complete in Ge‘ez (Ethiopic) and included in the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo canon; Aramaic fragments of several sections were found at Qumran among the Dead Sea Scrolls. The Parables/Similitudes (chapters 37–71) have not been found among those fragments, and their date remains debated. [1]

2 Enoch (Slavonic): A separate work, preserved in Old Church Slavonic; its date and origins are debated, and it has no known Qumran fragments.

3 Enoch (Hebrew): Much later (post–Temple, mystical/merkabah tradition); focuses on Enoch as Metatron.

Note: When we say “Enoch” here, we mean 1 Enoch, the collection quoted by Jude and preserved in the Ethiopian Orthodox canon.

3) What’s inside 1 Enoch (the five sections)

  • Book of the Watchers (1–36): Angels (Watchers) rebel, take wives, produce the Nephilim. They teach forbidden knowledge (weapons, sorcery/pharmakeia). God judges them; the Flood is announced.
  • Parables/Similitudes (37–71): Visions of judgment and a messianic Son of Man enthroned to judge kings and the wicked (especially 1 Enoch 46, 48, and 62). The imagery draws on Daniel 7:13–14: a humanlike figure receives an everlasting kingdom and authority to judge. [3]
  • Astronomical Book (72–82): Calendar, luminaries, gates of sun and moon—appointed times overseen by angels.
  • Dream Visions (83–90): Symbolic history (“animal apocalypse”): nations/empires as animals; judgment scenes.
  • Epistle of Enoch (91–108): Woes to sinners, comfort to the righteous, week-by-week epochs leading to final judgment.

Was the Flood only about human wickedness? Genesis says human evil and violence filled the earth (Genesis 6:5–13). But 1 Enoch expands the brief Genesis 6 account: the Watchers cross the boundary between heaven and earth, take human wives, and produce the giants; they also teach destructive knowledge, and violence spreads. In 1 Enoch 10:7–11, the earth is described as corrupted by the angels and the secrets they taught, and God orders the earth healed while Noah is warned. [2]

I wonder if the Flood was not simply because humans were wicked, but also because the Watchers had brought something into the human world that was never meant to be there. The angels themselves are God’s creation; what they do is rebel against their place, and the offspring and violence that follow represent a corruption of the order God made. To me, the Flood reads as God cleansing a world damaged by both human evil and this supernatural rebellion. That is my reading of Genesis alongside Enoch—not a claim that Genesis gives only one cause, since Genesis itself names human wickedness directly.

4) Why Enoch mattered to early believers

Cultural context: 1 Enoch was read and copied in some Jewish communities before and around the time of Jesus; fragments survived at Qumran. New Testament echoes: Jude quotes it; 2 Peter and Jude both refer to sinning angels in language that resonates with the Watchers tradition (2 Peter 2:4; Jude 6, 14–15). Messiah language: the “Son of Man” and throne-judgment imagery invite comparison with Daniel, the Gospels, and Revelation.

The Son of Man is not a throwaway title in Enoch. In these visions he is chosen, associated with righteousness, revealed before the kings, and seated in glory to judge them. When I read that beside Daniel 7 and Jesus’ words in the Gospels—especially Mark 14:61–62—I see a powerful line pointing to Christ. To me, Enoch’s Son of Man is evidence for Jesus, not a reason to dismiss the book. [3]

There is a dating question scholars debate: the Parables have not been found among the Qumran Enoch fragments, so those fragments do not by themselves prove that this section predates Jesus. I still find the connection spiritually striking. Whether a reader sees prophecy, shared Jewish tradition, or later literary development, the Son of Man passages deserve to be read directly rather than brushed aside.

5) Why it isn’t in your Bible (and why Ethiopia kept it)

There was no single moment when one council removed 1 Enoch from a Bible shared by every community. Different Jewish and Christian traditions transmitted different collections of scripture. 1 Enoch remained scripture in the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, which preserved the complete text in Ge‘ez; it was not included in most later Jewish, Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, or Protestant canons. Its presence among the Qumran manuscripts and Jude’s direct quotation show that it mattered in ancient Jewish and early Christian settings. [1]

My honest belief is that the Son of Man vision is one reason Enoch became inconvenient and was pushed aside: it presents a figure whose authority and judgment look strikingly like the Christ revealed in the Gospels. I cannot point to a surviving decree that proves this was the reason, and Enoch’s reception has a complex history. But I do believe the book’s witness to Jesus deserves to be taken seriously—not dismissed as dangerous or irrelevant.

6) The headline themes (and why they hit today)

  • The Watchers & Forbidden Knowledge: spiritual rebellion teaches tech/sorcery that accelerates violence and corruption. My modern echo: weaponized knowledge, AI and bioethics, drug culture, and surveillance. These are present-day applications of Enoch’s warning, not technologies named by the ancient text.
  • The Nephilim & Hybridization: crossing God-given boundaries produces chaos. My modern echo: transhumanism and moral drift—a present-day analogy to the boundary-crossing story, not a direct prediction in Enoch.
  • The Son of Man & Final Judgment: a divine ruler brings justice, overturns corrupt thrones. Today’s echo: global systems look untouchable—until the Judge arrives (Rev 11:15).
  • Times & Seasons: appointed times vs manipulated cycles. Today’s echo: information control and Babylon 2.0’s narrative clocks.

7) Enoch + Babylon 2.0 + the Trumpets (connecting the dots)

Babylon 2.0 = the system (centralized power + deception). Enoch = the backstory (how spiritual rebellion seeded tech/sorcery/control). Trumpets = the alarms (judgments that unwind the system Enoch said would rise).

8) How to read Enoch safely (simple guardrails)

  • Treat it as ancient context, not new doctrine. Let the Bible interpret Enoch, not the other way around.
  • Notice where the New Testament affirms Enoch’s ideas (Jude; 2 Peter; Revelation’s “Son of Man” scenes).
  • Watch the pattern: pride → deception → tech-as-sorcery → oppression → judgment → rescue.

9) Quick FAQ

Is Enoch Scripture? Canonical in Ethiopian Orthodoxy; valuable/quoted in early Christianity; not in most Protestant/Catholic/Orthodox canons.

Did Jesus read Enoch? We can’t prove He cited it, but its “Son of Man” and judgment imagery saturate His teaching.

Why does Jude quote it? Second-Temple Jews treated it as authoritative background; quoting it doesn’t canonize the entire book—just as Paul quotes Greek poets.

10) The Takeaway

Enoch doesn’t compete with the Bible; it spotlights the Bible’s warnings. It names the source code of deception—fallen intelligences teaching humanity to build a counterfeit kingdom. That counterfeit is what we called Babylon 2.0. And as the Trumpets sound, the system Enoch warned about shakes.

If you sense modern life feels engineered, Enoch says: that pattern started long ago—with watchers, forbidden knowledge, and a counterfeit wisdom that looks bright but enslaves.
