The Ethiopian collection

Enoch and Jubilees are not treated as disposable footnotes here. They are part of the Ethiopian Orthodox biblical tradition, and their narratives have their own entries, connections, and full reading texts in the atlas.

Book counts depend on the edition and on how works are grouped. The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church’s own published canon explanation describes 81 books. English collections marketed as “88 books” do not all have an identical table of contents.

Coverage note: the project’s requested reference scope is an 88-book Ethiopian collection. Its exact edition has not yet been matched book for book. This atlas is therefore not represented as a verified complete 88-book Bible. Its reading room contains the named translations below, and the timeline is a curated Genesis–Kings journey, not an entry for every episode in every book.

The reading texts

World English Bible Classic

A public-domain English translation, using its ecumenical book set. It supplies a readable English text for the shared biblical books; it is not a translation of the complete Ethiopian canon.

The reader keeps chapter and verse divisions. Website navigation, ordinary footnotes, and markup are omitted. When a verse exists only in a textual note in this edition, that note is clearly labeled in the reader; the source edition remains available for all other notes.

Summaries & connections

Atlas summaries and guided journeys are newly written editorial material. They point to primary passages rather than replacing them. A related-entry link can identify a person, shared location, parallel account, or thematic connection; it does not make all those relationships identical.

Open the passage buttons to compare accounts directly. A text’s story setting, its theological interpretation, and its historical composition are separate questions.

Two ways of telling time

The story view follows narrative order. Creation, Enoch, the Flood, the ancestral stories, and the Exodus are not assigned invented BCE dates. Events that overlap or are retold in another book remain connected without pretending there is only one possible chronological reconstruction.

The kings view uses a stated display convention. For the divided monarchy it follows the Thiele column in the comparison of royal chronologies, with broadly conventional early-monarchy estimates. The brief reign of Jehoiachin is displayed across 598–597 BCE to include the conventional 597 capture. Boundaries, accession-year counting, co-regencies, and rival claims produce differences among chronologies. The bars are a navigation aid, not a new harmonization of every regnal total.

Very short reigns receive visible minimum-width markers; this does not turn seven days or three months into a full year. Tibni is identified as a rival claimant. Ish-bosheth’s contested rule is explained in his entry and in the early-monarchy note. Jerusalem’s fall is commonly dated to 587 or 586 BCE; the display uses 586.

Prophets have broad contextual windows. A name appearing beside a selected year means “explore this wider period,” not “this prophet is independently known to have spoken in that exact year.” Where a book supplies named kings or a regnal year, the entry cites it.

Family lines are not all the same

Solid lines show parent-to-child connections; dotted lines show marriages; long dashes show an ancestral connection with omitted generations. Trees are selected, sourced family views, not exhaustive pedigrees. The twelve-tribes diagram groups children under their mothers, with Jacob as father of each child shown.

Repeated names are disambiguated: Enoch in Seth’s line is not Cain’s son; Lamech, Noah’s father, is not the Lamech of Genesis 4; similarly named rulers in Israel and Judah remain separate people. Ambiguous kinship is described rather than forced into a precise line—for example, Athaliah’s place in the house of Omri.

A landscape, not a claim of certainty

The atlas uses public-domain Natural Earth land shapes and editorially selected approximate coordinates. It does not draw modern political borders or reconstruct ancient coastlines. City pins provide orientation; regional and disputed identifications are explicitly marked.

Eden, visionary realms, and an exact sea-crossing site are not assigned invented coordinates. The Sinai pin is the traditional Jebel Musa identification and is marked disputed. Ararat is a regional marker, not proof of an ark landing site. Dashed connections between places are not reconstructed roads or travel routes.

Made for the bucket

The illustrated story landscape, interface, narrative journeys, and diagrams were created for BibleBucket. The illustration is an original SVG stored with the site. Natural Earth map data is public domain. No remote map service, advertising script, or login is required.

Saved entries stay in your browser’s local storage. Shared links encode a selected entry and view, not a personal account. The reading texts are hosted alongside the atlas and loaded on demand; a first visit still needs a connection to the website.
